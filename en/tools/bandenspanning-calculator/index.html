Tip: this is a good starting point, not a law set in stone. Ride it, fine-tune by feel and never go above the maximum pressure printed on the sidewall of your tyre.

How is this number worked out?
  • Base: 30 psi at the rear for 85 kg in total (you + bike) on 40 mm tyres. Heavier = more pressure, in proportion. Wider tyre = less pressure: 45 mm comes out about 16% lower than 40 mm.
  • Front tyre: 10% lower than the rear, because it carries less weight.
  • Terrain: smooth 15% higher, rough 15% lower.
  • Wheel size: 650b 5% higher than 700c.
  • Inner tube: 15% higher than tubeless, to avoid pinch flats.
  • Rounded to half a psi and always between 15 and 60 psi.

This is a Gravel Gorilla rule of thumb, not a measurement. Casing, rim width and your own feel decide the last few psi.

About this calculator

Too much tyre pressure and you rattle over every root. You lose grip as soon as it gets loose and every bump goes straight through your hands. Too little and you pinch-flat yourself, or you drag a soft, slow tyre along. The difference between right and wrong is usually a few tenths of a bar. Yet hardly anyone actually stops to work it out.

Our tyre pressure calculator does the maths for you. Enter your weight, including luggage, the width of your tyre (from a fast 28 mm to a wide 55 mm), your wheel size, valve type and the surface you ride on. You immediately get advice per wheel in both psi and bar. Your front tyre automatically gets about 10% less than your rear, because that gives just enough grip in corners without the front washing out. Outside the usual range for gravel tyres? Then the tool warns you. Better that than leaving the car park with far too much or too little air.

On sandy forest trails you need that advice more than anywhere. From smooth "champagne gravel" to a deep strip of loose sand in a dry summer: every surface asks for a different pressure. The same trails can also be full of roots and hidden holes, which punish a tyre that's too soft with a hit against the rim. Set the surface to "rough", enter your tyre width and weight, and the calculator balances that contrast for you. Still riding with inner tubes? Then the tool adds an extra correction, because a pinch flat from a hard hit on a root is not a pleasant break in your ride.

Set it before you leave, fine-tune by feel in the first kilometres, and you no longer have to guess on the way.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good tyre pressure for a gravel bike?

That depends on your weight, the width of your tyre and the surface. As an example: for a 75 kg rider on 40 mm tubeless tyres on mixed surfaces, the calculator comes to about 1.8 bar front and 2.0 bar rear. Heavier, narrower tyres or inner tubes mean more pressure; wider tyres or loose sand mean less.

Why should my front tyre be softer than my rear?

Your rear wheel carries most of your weight. A front tyre with about 10 percent less pressure gives more grip in corners and more comfort in your hands, without the front washing out.

Tubeless or inner tube: does it matter for pressure?

Yes. With an inner tube the calculator adds an extra correction. A tyre that's too soft with an inner tube pinch-flats more easily: a puncture because the tube gets squeezed against the rim, for example when you hit a tree root.

Which tyres does the calculator work for?

For tyres from 28 to 55 mm wide, on 700c and 650b wheels, for riders from 45 to 130 kg including gear. You choose the surface (smooth, mixed or rough) and whether you ride tubeless or with inner tubes. You get the advice in bar and psi.

How do I measure my tyre pressure?

With a pump with a good gauge, or with a separate digital gauge. Tyres slowly lose air, especially tubeless ones. So check the pressure before every ride and fine-tune by feel after the first kilometres.
