Which chainring is best for a gravel bike?

With 1×, 40 teeth is the most popular choice: a good balance between climbing and speed. If you ride a lot in the hills or with luggage, 38 or even 36 is nicer. If you mainly ride fast and flat, 42 or 44 suits. Put your options side by side in the calculator to see the difference.

What does development mean?

Development (or roll-out) is how many metres your bike moves forward with one turn of the pedals. A small development (for example 2 metres) is light pedalling, handy uphill. A large development (8 metres or more) is heavy pedalling, for speed. It depends on your chainring, your rear sprocket and the circumference of your wheel with tyre.

How steep can I climb with my gears?

That depends on your lowest gear, your weight with bike and luggage, your power and your cadence. As an example: with 40 front and 42 rear, 90 kilos in total, 200 watts and 75 rpm you ride about 7 percent seated. Short, steeper sections work standing or at a lower cadence, but you can't keep that up for long. The calculator works it out for you.

What's the difference between a 10–44 and an 11–42 cassette?

A 10–44 has both a smaller sprocket (10) and a bigger one (44): you get a harder top gear and a lighter climbing gear. An 11–42 has less range. Note: a cassette with a 10-tooth sprocket only fits a matching freehub, and not every derailleur can handle a large sprocket.

Does any cassette fit my bike?

No. Your derailleur must handle the biggest sprocket, your freehub must fit (such as Shimano HG, Microspline or SRAM XDR) and the number of speeds must match your shifters and chain. Check the specifications of your components or ask your bike shop before you order anything.
